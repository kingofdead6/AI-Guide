Risk and empirical risk

$$R(f)=\mathbb E_{(\mathbf x,y)\sim P}[\mathcal L(y,f(\mathbf x))],\qquad \hat R(f)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathcal L(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i))$$

Empirical risk minimization picks \(f\) to minimize \(\hat R\). The generalization gap \(R-\hat R\) grows with model flexibility and shrinks with more data. Underfitting: both errors high. Overfitting: training error low, validation error high.

Bias–variance decomposition

For \(y=f(\mathbf x)+\varepsilon\) with noise variance \(\sigma^2\), and a model \(\hat f\) trained on a random dataset:

$$\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(\mathbf x))^2\big]=\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}+\underbrace{\big(f(\mathbf x)-\mathbb E[\hat f(\mathbf x)]\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(\hat f(\mathbf x)-\mathbb E[\hat f(\mathbf x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}$$

Simple models have high bias; flexible models have high variance. Regularization, more data, and ensembling lower variance. Richer features and bigger models lower bias. (Very large neural networks show "double descent": past the interpolation point, test error can fall again. Classical intuition still guides most practical decisions.)

Cross-validation

\(k\)-fold CV splits training data into \(k\) folds, trains on \(k-1\) and validates on the remaining one, rotating. The average score is a lower-variance estimate of generalization, used to choose hyperparameters (settings you pick, like depth or \(\lambda\)), as opposed to parameters (learned weights).

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, GridSearchCV
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
grid = GridSearchCV(RandomForestClassifier(random_state=0),
                    {"max_depth": [4, 8, None], "n_estimators": [200, 500]},
                    cv=5, scoring="roc_auc", n_jobs=-1)
grid.fit(X_tr, y_tr); print(grid.best_params_, grid.best_score_)
Curse of dimensionality

In high dimensions, data is sparse and distances become similar: for random points, the ratio of nearest to farthest distance tends toward 1. Methods that rely on "nearby points" (kNN, kernels) need far more data as \(d\) grows. Learned representations (embeddings) fix this by mapping data to spaces where distance is meaningful.
