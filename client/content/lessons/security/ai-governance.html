What governance means in practice

AI governance is the set of practices that make sure a system does what it should, for the people it affects, within the rules that apply. For an engineer it is concrete work: writing down intended use and limits, measuring risks, keeping records, and building the controls that let a team catch problems early.

A lightweight risk assessment

Before building, and again before launch, answer these in writing:

  1. Purpose and users: what decision or task does the system support, and who is affected, including people who never use it directly?
  2. Harms: what happens when it is wrong? Consider errors (false positives and negatives), unequal error rates across groups ([[responsible-ai]]), misuse, privacy, security and over-reliance.
  3. Severity and likelihood: rank each harm. A movie recommender's mistakes are cheap; a loan, hiring, medical or policing system's are not.
  4. Controls: for each serious harm, a mitigation (human review, thresholds, restricted scope, monitoring) and an owner.
  5. Go / no-go criteria: metrics and thresholds the system must meet before launch, decided in advance.

Documentation that teams actually use

  • Model cards: intended use and out-of-scope uses, training data summary, evaluation results overall and per relevant subgroup, known limitations, ethical considerations, and contact owner.
  • Datasheets for datasets: motivation, collection process, consent and licensing, preprocessing, known biases and recommended uses ([[data-centric]]).
  • System cards for LLM applications: the model, the prompts and tools, the safety mitigations and the results of red-teaming.
  • Decision logs: why thresholds, data sources and trade-offs were chosen, so future maintainers can revisit them.
# Model card: Support-ticket triage classifier v3
Intended use: route incoming tickets to one of 8 queues. A human agent can re-route.
Out of scope: deciding refunds or account closures.
Data: 120k labelled tickets (2024-2026), English, French and Arabic; PII removed before training.
Evaluation (held-out, time-split): macro-F1 0.86; Arabic 0.81, French 0.85, English 0.88.
Limitations: weaker on Darija written in Latin script; new product names are misrouted until retraining.
Monitoring: weekly per-language F1 on a reviewed sample; alert if any language drops by 5 points.
Owner: ML platform team.

The regulatory landscape

Rules for AI are developing quickly and differ by country, so treat this section as orientation and check current official sources for anything you ship.

  • Risk-based regulation: the European Union's AI Act sorts systems by risk. A small set of practices is prohibited; "high-risk" uses (for example in hiring, credit, education and critical infrastructure) carry obligations such as risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and robustness; some systems have transparency duties (people must know they are talking to an AI or viewing generated content); and general-purpose AI model providers have their own obligations. Its requirements phase in over several years.
  • Data protection law such as the GDPR (and many national laws modelled on similar principles) already applies whenever personal data is processed: lawful basis, purpose limitation, data minimization, rights of access and erasure, and rules on solely automated decisions with significant effects.
  • Frameworks and standards: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (govern, map, measure, manage) and ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management systems) give voluntary structures many organizations adopt.
  • Sector rules: medicine, finance, insurance and employment often have their own requirements for validation, explainability and record-keeping.
Not legal adviceThis lesson explains the shape of the landscape so you can ask the right questions. For a real product, involve legal or compliance specialists and read the current texts that apply in your jurisdiction.

Building governance into the workflow

  • Make the risk assessment and model card part of the definition of done, reviewed like code.
  • Automate the evidence: evaluation reports, per-group metrics and data lineage generated by the training pipeline ([[mlops]]).
  • Keep a human in the loop where errors are costly, and design the interface so the human can actually disagree (show confidence, sources and the reason for a decision).
  • Plan incident response: how users report problems, who triages them, how to roll back a model.
  • Re-assess when the data, the model, the users or the purpose change.
Practice problems
  1. Which of these needs the heaviest controls: a playlist recommender, an internal code-search assistant, or a CV-screening model? Why?
    Solution
    The CV-screening model. Its errors affect people's access to jobs, can encode discrimination, and fall into categories that regulation commonly treats as high-risk, so it needs bias evaluation, documentation, human oversight and legal review.
  2. Write the two most important lines of a model card for a toxicity filter.
    Solution
    For example: "Intended use: flag comments for human review; not for automatic account bans." and "Known limitation: higher false-positive rate on dialectal Arabic and reclaimed slurs; see per-language results."
