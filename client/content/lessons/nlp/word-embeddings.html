One-hot vectors are huge and every pair is orthogonal ("cat" is as far from "kitten" as from "carburetor"). Embeddings map each word to \(\mathbf v_w\in\mathbb R^{d}\) with \(d\) around 100–300.

Skip-gram

Predict context words \(o\) from a center word \(c\), with separate center vectors \(\mathbf v\) and context vectors \(\mathbf u\):

$$p(o\mid c)=\frac{\exp(\mathbf u_o^\top\mathbf v_c)}{\sum_{w\in V}\exp(\mathbf u_w^\top\mathbf v_c)}$$

The denominator sums over the whole vocabulary, which is too expensive. Negative sampling turns it into binary classification: real pairs versus \(k\) random "noise" words:

$$\mathcal L=-\log\sigma(\mathbf u_o^\top\mathbf v_c)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\sigma(-\mathbf u_{n_j}^\top\mathbf v_c)$$

GloVe instead fits embeddings to global co-occurrence statistics: \(\mathbf v_i^\top\mathbf u_j+b_i+b_j\approx\log X_{ij}\). Both produce linear structure: \(\mathbf v_{\text{king}}-\mathbf v_{\text{man}}+\mathbf v_{\text{woman}}\approx\mathbf v_{\text{queen}}\).

LimitationOne vector per word means "bank" (river) and "bank" (money) share a vector. Contextual embeddings from Transformers (6.4) give each occurrence its own vector. Embeddings also absorb social biases present in their training text.
from gensim.models import Word2Vec
sentences = [["the", "cat", "sat"], ["the", "dog", "ran"]]          # tokenized corpus
w2v = Word2Vec(sentences, vector_size=100, window=5, min_count=1, sg=1, negative=5)
print(w2v.wv.most_similar("cat"))
